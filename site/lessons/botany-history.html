A connected timeline

From useful plants to plant science

Plant knowledge did not begin with one European scientist. Communities around the world developed detailed knowledge of edible, poisonous, medicinal, fiber, dye, and crop plants. Written botanical works preserve only a portion of the much larger history of observation and practice.

Ancient worldTheophrastus (c. 371–287 BCE) described plant forms, growth, and uses in works including Enquiry into Plants.
Many generationsHerbal traditions organized plants by their practical uses. Knowledge was passed through observation, cultivation, and written works across cultures.
1500sEuropean botanists developed plant gardens and herbaria: collections of dried, labeled specimens that could be compared and studied later.
1700sCarl Linnaeus helped standardize two-part scientific names. Species Plantarum (1753) became a key starting point for modern plant naming.
1800s–todayPlant science expanded through cell biology, physiology, genetics, ecology, evolution, microscopy, chemistry, and DNA-based classification.

These stages overlap. Practical knowledge, classification, and experiments developed in many places and did not simply replace one another.

Tools and questions

What changed as botanists studied plants?

ApproachQuestion it helps answerEvidence or tool
Ethnobotany and cultivationHow do people use, manage, and name plants?Community knowledge, field observations, crop histories, and interviews conducted with respect and permission.
Herbarium and field botanyIs this specimen the same species as another one?Pressed plants with collection location, date, habitat, and identifier.
TaxonomyHow should organisms be named and grouped?Shared diagnostic traits, formal names, and reference specimens.
Experimental plant biologyWhat causes a plant response or growth pattern?Controlled experiments, measurements, microscopy, and repeatable observations.
Evolutionary systematicsWhich groups share ancestors?Comparisons of anatomy, fossils, development, and DNA.
Important distinction: Linnaeus’s naming system made plant names more consistent, but his original groupings were not modern evolutionary trees. Today, classification aims to reflect shared ancestry and can change when new evidence appears.
A short research assignment

Create a one-page history of botany

  1. Draw a horizontal timeline with five stops: ancient plant study, herbal knowledge, herbaria and botanical gardens, Linnaean naming, and modern molecular botany.
  2. For each stop, write one question people were trying to answer and one kind of evidence they used.
  3. Add a plant specimen, drawing, or photograph. Include its common name, scientific name if known, and where the identification came from.
  4. Choose one crop or medicinal plant. Explain how practical knowledge and formal research each contribute to what we know about it.
  5. End with a sentence about why plant names and classifications can change as evidence improves.

Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. Why is Theophrastus remembered in botany?

His surviving ancient works systematically described plants, their growth, and their uses.

2. What does a herbarium add to field observation?

It preserves labeled specimens so researchers can compare plants across places and time.

3. Did Linnaeus create modern evolutionary classification?

No. He helped standardize scientific names and classification, but evolutionary relationships were not the basis of his original system.

4. What is one limitation of a written history of botany?

Written records preserve only part of plant knowledge; many communities’ practical knowledge was oral, local, or excluded from formal scientific histories.

  • Explain the difference between a herbarium specimen and a living botanical garden.
  • Give one example of how botany uses evidence from different fields.
  • Describe how modern DNA data can revise plant classification.
