Where Plants Move and Store Resources

Plants move water, minerals, sugars, and gases through tissues and air spaces. They also store carbon-rich food, water, and mineral nutrients in cells, seeds, and specialized organs. Use this page to connect transport pathways to the places resources are used or kept.

Movement through the body

Transport is not the same as storage

ResourceMain movementCommon destination or use
Water and mineral ionsRoots absorb them; xylem carries water and dissolved minerals upward through roots, stems, and leaves.Photosynthesis, cell expansion, cooling, and metabolism; excess water can leave as vapor.
SugarsLeaves and other sources load sugars into phloem; sugars move toward sinks.Growing shoots, roots, fruits, seeds, and storage organs.
Carbon dioxide and oxygenDiffuse through stomata, lenticels, and internal air spaces; they move down concentration gradients.CO2 supplies photosynthesis; O2 is used in respiration and is also released by photosynthesis.
Mineral nutrientsMove in xylem with water, enter cells through membranes, and may be redistributed through phloem.Build proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, membranes, and other cell structures.

Source means a tissue releasing sugars to phloem; sink means a tissue using or storing them. A leaf may be a source when mature, then a sink while it is growing.

Reserves for later

Cells and organs can act as stores

Seeds

Embryos are packaged with reserves in endosperm or cotyledons. These reserves fuel germination before the seedling can photosynthesize enough.

Roots and stems

Carrots and beets store reserves in roots; potato tubers are modified stems; rhizomes and bulbs also store resources and support regrowth.

Leaves and cells

Many cells store starch in plastids and solutes or ions in vacuoles. Some leaves store water, especially in succulent plants.

Fruits and trunks

Fruits may contain sugars and oils; woody stems store carbon in wood and living tissues. Storage location varies by species and season.

Plants often convert transported sucrose into storage compounds such as starch or oils. Stored reserves can support winter survival, flowering, regrowth, or the next generation.

Gases inside plant bodies

Plants exchange gases; they rarely store them as reserves

Most plants do not keep a large reserve of oxygen or carbon dioxide in the way seeds store fats or starch. Gas exchange happens mainly by diffusion. Stomata regulate the trade: opening them helps carbon dioxide enter but also lets water vapor escape. Leaves contain intercellular air spaces that let gases reach photosynthetic cells.

Some wetland plants form aerenchyma, tissue with large air spaces that helps oxygen move to submerged roots. CAM plants temporarily store captured carbon as organic acids, often in vacuoles overnight, then release CO2 for daytime photosynthesis. This is temporary carbon storage in a chemical form, not a gas tank.

Useful station distinction: a stomatal pore and leaf air spaces are gas-exchange pathways; aerenchyma is an internal gas-conduction adaptation; starch, oils, water, and mineral ions are examples of stored reserves.
Make a pathway diagram

Trace one resource through a plant

  1. Pick water, nitrate, carbon dioxide, or sugar.
  2. Draw where it enters or is produced and label the tissue or structure that carries it.
  3. Show where it is used, released, or stored. If it is stored, name the compound or organ.
  4. For water, connect root uptake, xylem transport, leaf evaporation, and stomatal control.
  5. For sugar, connect photosynthesis, phloem source-to-sink movement, cellular respiration, and starch/oil storage.

Use arrows to show direction, and annotate whether movement occurs by bulk flow, diffusion, osmosis, or active loading.

Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. Which tissue carries most water from roots toward shoots?

Xylem, driven mainly by transpiration and cohesion-tension in most tall plants.

2. What makes a tissue a phloem sink?

It is using or storing sugars delivered from a source.

3. Name two specialized storage organs.

Examples include potato tubers (stems), carrot roots, rhizomes, bulbs, seeds, and some fleshy leaves.

4. Do plants generally store oxygen gas as a long-term reserve?

No. They exchange gases and maintain internal air spaces; some species have adaptations such as aerenchyma for gas movement. Food reserves are stored mainly as chemical compounds.

  • Distinguish transport tissues from storage tissues and organs.
  • Trace water, minerals, and sugars through the plant body.
  • Explain why gas exchange trades CO2 uptake against water loss.
