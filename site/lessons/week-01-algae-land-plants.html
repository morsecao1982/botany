Part 1 · Eukaryote diversity

What are protists?

Protist is a useful name for a wide variety of eukaryotes that are not plants, animals, or fungi in the familiar sense. Most are single-celled, but some—such as seaweeds—are large and multicellular. The group is extremely diverse: some make sugars using light, some eat or absorb food, and some can do both.

Important classification idea: “Protists” do not all descend from one exclusive branch of the tree of life. It is a convenient umbrella name, not one natural kingdom whose members are all close relatives.

Biologists compare cell structures, life cycles, and DNA to infer relationships. A common way to organize eukaryotes in this reading is into four large groups. Scientists continue to revise these groupings as new evidence arrives, so focus on recognizing the examples and traits emphasized here.

Some protists matter far beyond their size

  • Photosynthetic protists, including many algae, are important producers in aquatic food webs. They make organic food from carbon dioxide and light, and many release oxygen.
  • Mutualists benefit their partners. Other protists are parasites that harm a host; for example, some oomycetes cause devastating plant diseases.
  • Diatoms and other plankton respond to available nutrients. Changes in their populations can affect the organisms that eat them and the amount of carbon moving through aquatic ecosystems.
Do all protists have the same life cycle?

No. Some reproduce asexually by cell division, while others also reproduce sexually. In some algae, multicellular haploid and diploid stages alternate. Other protists have very different cycles; malaria-causing Plasmodium, for example, changes form as it moves between a mosquito and a human host.

A closer look

Algae are diverse—and “alga” does not mean “land plant”

Algae are photosynthetic organisms that generally live in water or damp places. The word describes a lifestyle more than a single evolutionary group. Red algae, green algae, and brown algae have different evolutionary histories. Some are single-celled; others are multicellular seaweeds.

Red algae

Their reddish pigment can help capture light underwater. Many are multicellular. They belong with green algae and plants in Archaeplastida.

Green algae

They share chlorophyll pigments and other traits with land plants. Charophyte algae are the closest living relatives of land plants.

Brown algae

These stramenopiles include kelps. A kelp’s blade may look like a leaf and its holdfast may look like a root, but they are not the same organs as a land plant’s leaves and roots.

Diatoms

Single-celled stramenopiles with silica “glass” walls. They are major aquatic producers and are often abundant in plankton.

Why charophytes are considered close relatives: Scientists compare DNA from nuclei and chloroplasts, as well as shared traits such as cell-wall chemistry, cell division, and features of sperm. No single clue proves ancestry; many kinds of evidence point to the same relationship.
Plant or not? Being green and photosynthetic is not enough to make an organism a land plant. Green algae are close relatives, but land plants share a set of additional traits—especially protected embryos and spores, a waxy cuticle, and alternation of generations.

How did chloroplasts evolve?

In primary endosymbiosis, an early eukaryotic cell engulfed a photosynthetic cyanobacterium but did not digest it. Over a very long time, the bacterium became a chloroplast. The descendants of this partnership include red algae, green algae, and land plants. Later, some other eukaryotes engulfed a red or green alga; this is called secondary endosymbiosis and helps explain why some protists have plastids surrounded by extra membranes.

A similar older partnership explains mitochondria: an ancestor of eukaryotes took in a bacterium that eventually became the mitochondrion. These partnerships are examples of how cooperation can shape evolution.

Part 2 · The move onto land

What challenges did plants face?

Water supports cells and lets sperm swim. On land, early plants also faced drying, stronger sunlight, gravity, and the need to move water and nutrients through a body. Different adaptations helped solve different problems.

There is no single “land adaptation.” A waxy cuticle helps prevent drying, but it also makes gas exchange harder. Stomata solve that problem by opening when the plant can take in carbon dioxide and closing when conserving water is more important.

A simple plant timeline

About 470 million years agoPlants arise from a lineage of green algae.
About 425 million years agoEarly vascular plants appear in the fossil record.
About 385 million years agoEarly forests form, dominated by ancient plant groups.
About 360 million years agoSeed plants appear; seeds later help plants reproduce without free-standing water for sperm to swim.

Fossil spores, stems, and other remains let scientists compare ancient plants with living groups. During the Carboniferous period, huge forests stored carbon in plant material. Some of that material became coal; changes in carbon storage affected Earth’s climate.

A cycle with two generations

Alternation of generations

Land plants alternate between two multicellular stages. The gametophyte is haploid (one set of chromosomes, written n) and makes eggs or sperm by mitosis. After fertilization, the diploid (two sets, 2n) zygote grows into a sporophyte. The sporophyte makes haploid spores by meiosis. Each spore can grow into a new gametophyte.

Gametophyte (n)Makes eggs and sperm by mitosis.
FertilizationEgg and sperm join to form a diploid zygote (2n).
Sporophyte (2n)Grows from the zygote and makes sporangia.
MeiosisInside a sporangium, the sporophyte makes haploid spores (n).
Spore (n)Grows by mitosis into a new gametophyte.
Do not mix up spores and sperm/eggs: spores grow into a new plant stage without joining another cell. Sperm and eggs must fuse during fertilization. In plants, meiosis makes spores—not gametes.
Why can algae have “alternation of generations” too?

Some algae also alternate between multicellular haploid and diploid stages. But the land-plant life cycle includes the special feature of a multicellular, dependent embryo that remains protected on the parent gametophyte.

Mosses, ferns, and the rise of vascular plants

One evolutionary detail: Seedless vascular plants include two major lineages—lycophytes and monilophytes. They both reproduce with spores, but they do not form one exclusive branch by themselves; each is related to other plant lineages in a branching evolutionary tree.

How mosses and ferns reproduce

  • Mosses: the green moss carpet is mostly gametophytes. Sperm form in antheridia; eggs form in archegonia. After fertilization, a sporophyte grows from the gametophyte, with a foot, stalk (seta), and capsule (sporangium). Spores leave the capsule and can grow into new moss gametophytes.
  • Ferns: the leafy fern is the sporophyte. Spore-producing structures often appear in clusters called sori on the underside of fronds. A spore grows into a small gametophyte; sperm swim through water to an egg, and a new leafy sporophyte grows after fertilization.
  • Leaf clues: lycophytes have small leaves called microphylls, usually with one unbranched vein. Ferns and seed plants have megaphylls, whose larger blades have branched veins. These are useful clues when comparing plant groups.
  • Heterospory: some vascular plants make two kinds of spores—small microspores that develop into male gametophytes and larger megaspores that develop into female gametophytes. This separation evolved in some seedless plants and was important in the origin of seeds.

Why mosses and early forests matter

Peat moss can hold a great deal of water, and peatlands store large amounts of carbon. Early vascular forests changed habitats and the carbon cycle; their buried remains helped form coal. These examples connect plant evolution to ecosystems and human resources.

Retrieval practice

Check yourself

Answer first, then open the explanation.

1. Why isn’t kelp considered a true land plant even though it has leaflike blades?

Kelp is a brown alga in the stramenopile group. Its blade only looks like a leaf; it is not the same specialized organ, and kelp does not share the defining land-plant life cycle and embryo features.

2. Which algae are closest to land plants?

Certain charophyte green algae. Shared cell and molecular traits support this relationship.

3. Put these in order: spore, sporophyte, gametophyte, zygote.

Gametophyte makes gametes → fertilization makes zygote → zygote grows into sporophyte → meiosis in a sporangium makes spores → spores grow into gametophytes.

4. Why can a fern grow taller than a moss?

Ferns have vascular tissue, including strengthened xylem, that moves water and helps support a taller body. Mosses lack true vascular tissue.

5. Which plant generation makes spores, and how are they made?

The diploid sporophyte makes haploid spores by meiosis, usually inside a sporangium.

6. Why do many mosses and ferns need wet conditions to reproduce?

Their sperm are flagellated and must swim through a film of water to reach an egg.
