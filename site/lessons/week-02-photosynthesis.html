How Plants Turn Light into Food

Photosynthesis feeds ecosystems

Photosynthesis captures energy from light and stores some of it in sugars. Plants are photoautotrophs: they use light energy, carbon dioxide, and water to build organic molecules. Animals and fungi are heterotrophs; they get energy and carbon by consuming other organisms or their products.

Net equation: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2. This is a useful summary, not a single-step recipe. The oxygen released comes from water molecules split during the light reactions.

Photosynthesis stores energy in chemical bonds; cellular respiration can later transfer energy from sugars into ATP. Sugars also provide carbon building blocks for cellulose, oils, proteins, and other parts of a plant.

Where it happens

Inside the chloroplast

PartWhat to remember
ThylakoidA flattened membrane sac containing pigments, photosystems, electron carriers, and ATP synthase. The light reactions occur here.
Granum (plural: grana)A stack of thylakoids that creates a large membrane surface area.
StromaThe fluid surrounding thylakoids. Enzymes here run the Calvin cycle.
StomataAdjustable pores in leaves. Carbon dioxide enters through them; oxygen and water vapor can leave.

Leaves have many chloroplast-filled mesophyll cells. A leaf’s air spaces help carbon dioxide move toward these cells, while veins deliver water and carry sugars away.

Stage 1

Light reactions: capture energy and move electrons

A pigment absorbs some wavelengths of light and reflects others. Chlorophyll a is the main reaction-center pigment; accessory pigments absorb additional wavelengths and pass the energy along. Because green wavelengths are reflected more than red and blue ones, many leaves look green.

Photosystem IILight energizes electrons. Water is split to replace them, releasing oxygen and hydrogen ions.
Electron transportElectrons pass through carriers in the thylakoid membrane. Their energy helps build a hydrogen-ion gradient.
ATP synthaseHydrogen ions flow back across the membrane; ATP synthase uses that flow to make ATP.
Photosystem ILight energizes the electrons again. They help reduce NADP+ to NADPH.

ATP supplies energy and NADPH supplies high-energy electrons for the next stage. In linear electron flow, both photosystems work and the products are ATP, NADPH, and oxygen.

What is cyclic electron flow?

Electrons leaving photosystem I cycle back through part of the electron-transport chain. This pathway makes extra ATP, but not NADPH or oxygen. It helps balance the cell’s supply of ATP and NADPH.

Stage 2

The Calvin cycle: build carbon molecules

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. It uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to add carbon dioxide to an organic molecule and make a small sugar called G3P. The cycle has three linked parts:

  1. Carbon fixation: the enzyme rubisco attaches CO2 to RuBP, a five-carbon molecule. The unstable product splits into smaller molecules.
  2. Reduction: ATP and NADPH help convert those molecules into G3P.
  3. Regeneration: most G3P is rearranged, using ATP, to rebuild RuBP so the cycle can continue.
Carbon count: When 3 CO2 molecules enter, one net G3P molecule can leave. The other carbon-containing molecules regenerate the cycle’s starting material. Plants can use G3P to make glucose and many other organic molecules.

The Calvin cycle does not need darkness; it depends on ATP and NADPH, which are usually supplied by the light reactions.

When it is hot or dry

Why plants use C3, C4, and CAM pathways

Most plants first fix carbon in a three-carbon compound and are called C3 plants. On a hot, dry day, closing stomata conserves water but lowers the carbon dioxide inside the leaf. Rubisco may then attach oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. This starts photorespiration, which uses energy and releases carbon without making sugar.

StrategyHow it helpsExamples / setting
C3Uses the Calvin cycle directly; efficient in cool, moist conditions with open stomata.Many temperate plants, including wheat and rice.
C4First fixes CO2 into a four-carbon molecule, then concentrates CO2 near rubisco in bundle-sheath cells. This reduces photorespiration.Often advantageous in hot, sunny settings; corn and many grasses.
CAMOpens stomata at night, stores captured carbon in acids, and releases CO2 for the Calvin cycle during the day.Water-saving strategy in cacti, pineapples, and other succulents.
Remember the difference: C4 separates early carbon capture and the Calvin cycle mainly by space (different cell types). CAM separates them by time (night versus day).
Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. Where do the light reactions and Calvin cycle occur?

Light reactions: thylakoid membranes. Calvin cycle: stroma.

2. What are the products of linear electron flow?

ATP, NADPH, and oxygen. ATP and NADPH are used to build carbon molecules; oxygen is released.

3. Why does photorespiration increase when a C3 plant closes its stomata on a hot day?

Less CO2 enters and oxygen becomes relatively more available, so rubisco is more likely to bind oxygen.

4. How is CAM different from C4?

Both concentrate carbon dioxide to reduce photorespiration. C4 separates the steps by cell type; CAM separates them by time of day.

  • Explain how light energy becomes ATP and NADPH, then helps make G3P.
  • Compare C3, C4, and CAM without mixing up space and time.
