Seeds, Pollen, Flowers, and the Fossil Record

See how seeds and pollen helped plants reproduce on land, compare gymnosperms and flowering plants, and use fossils to tell the story of plant evolution.

Why seeds changed plant life

From spores to seeds

Seed plants still alternate between haploid gametophytes and diploid sporophytes, but the gametophytes became very small and stayed protected inside the sporophyte. A seed contains a young plant embryo, a protective coat, and stored food. It can wait through harsh conditions and travel before it germinates.

InnovationWhat it contributes
HeterosporyTwo spore sizes: microspores develop into male gametophytes; megaspores develop into female gametophytes.
OvuleA megasporangium and female gametophyte protected by integuments. After fertilization, the ovule develops into a seed.
PollenContains the reduced male gametophyte. It carries sperm-producing cells to the ovule without needing sperm to swim through outside water.
SeedPackages the embryo with a food supply and protective coat. Dormancy helps time germination for favorable conditions.

These changes let seed plants reproduce in drier places than mosses and ferns, whose sperm generally need a film of water to reach an egg.

Seed plants without flowers

Gymnosperms: “naked seeds”

Gymnosperm means “naked seed”: the ovules are not enclosed in an ovary. In many gymnosperms, seeds develop on the scales of cones. The sporophyte is the large tree or shrub; the gametophytes are microscopic and depend on it.

Conifers

Pines, spruces, firs, and redwoods. Many have woody cones, needlelike or scalelike leaves, and are evergreen.

Cycads

Palmlike plants with large cones. They are an ancient seed-plant lineage.

Ginkgo

Ginkgo biloba is the only living species in its group; its fan-shaped leaves and fleshy seed coverings are distinctive.

Gnetophytes

A small, unusual group of gymnosperms with several very different-looking members.

Fossils show that gymnosperms were especially widespread during parts of the Mesozoic Era. They remain important in forests today.

Seeds enclosed in ovaries

Angiosperms: flowers and fruits

Angiosperms are flowering plants. Flowers organize pollination and fertilization; fruits form from ovaries and help protect or disperse seeds.

Four flower parts to identify
PartJob
SepalOften protects the flower bud before it opens.
PetalOften helps attract pollinators.
StamenMale flower part. Anthers make pollen; the stalk is the filament.
CarpelFemale flower part. The stigma receives pollen, the style connects to the ovary, and ovules sit inside the ovary.

After pollen lands on a compatible stigma, it grows a pollen tube down toward an ovule. Two sperm travel through the tube. In double fertilization, one sperm joins the egg to make a diploid zygote, which develops into the embryo. The other joins the central cell’s polar nuclei to form endosperm, which nourishes the embryo. The ovule becomes a seed; the ovary usually becomes a fruit.

Monocots and eudicots: These are two major angiosperm groups. Monocot embryos have one cotyledon (seed leaf); eudicot embryos have two. Leaf veins, flower-part numbers, and stem vascular-bundle patterns can also help identify them, but no single visible clue is perfect.

Flowering plants diversified rapidly and now dominate many land ecosystems. Pollinators and seed-dispersing animals can benefit from plant food, while plants benefit when pollen or seeds are moved.

Reading deep time

What paleobotany learns from fossils

Paleobotany is the study of ancient plants and plant fossils. Fossils preserve evidence such as spores, leaves, wood, and reproductive structures. Scientists compare fossil ages and forms with living plants and use evolutionary trees to infer when traits appeared. The fossil record is incomplete, so conclusions combine fossils with anatomy, chemistry, and DNA from living species.

~470 million years agoEarly land plants evolve from green-algal relatives.
~425 million years agoEarly vascular plants appear.
~385 million years agoEarly forests develop.
~360 million years agoSeed plants appear.
~140 million years agoFlowering plants arise and later become widespread.

Mass extinctions and climate shifts changed which groups were common. Fossils can reveal extinct lineages and intermediate combinations of traits. Ancient forests also changed the carbon cycle; buried plant material contributed to coal deposits.

Why plant diversity matters to people: A small number of seed-plant species supply much of the world’s food, while plants also provide medicines and other useful materials. Losing plant species can mean losing resources and evolutionary options before we learn what they could provide.
Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. How do pollen and seeds reduce plants’ dependence on water?

Pollen carries the male gametophyte to the ovule, so sperm do not need an outside film of water to swim. Seeds protect and nourish an embryo and can remain dormant.

2. What becomes a seed, and what usually becomes a fruit?

The fertilized ovule becomes a seed; the ovary usually develops into a fruit.

3. What happens during double fertilization?

One sperm fertilizes the egg to form the embryo’s zygote; a second sperm fuses with polar nuclei to form food-storing endosperm.

4. What evidence can paleobotanists use to study plant evolution?

Fossil spores, leaves, wood, and reproductive structures, compared with the traits and DNA of living plants.

  • Distinguish a gymnosperm from an angiosperm.
  • Label sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels.
  • Explain the ovule-to-seed and ovary-to-fruit changes.
  • Put the major plant innovations on a timeline.
