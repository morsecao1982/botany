Flowers, Seeds, and Plant Biotechnology

Follow an angiosperm from flower to seed, compare sexual and asexual reproduction, and learn how people change crop plants through breeding and genetic engineering.

The reproductive shoot

Four flower parts to know

A flower is a reproductive structure made by the diploid sporophyte. Its parts are modified leaves arranged in a pattern around the center.

PartWhere to lookFunction
SepalsOuter, often green layer.Protect the flower bud before it opens.
PetalsOften colorful and showy.Can attract animal pollinators.
StamensFilament topped by anther.Anthers produce pollen, which contains the reduced male gametophyte.
CarpelStigma, style, and ovary.The stigma receives pollen; the ovary contains ovules with the female gametophyte.

Not every flower has all four parts or has both male and female parts. Some flowers are incomplete or unisexual; some species have male and female flowers on separate plants.

From pollen to fruit

Pollination and double fertilization

PollinationPollen lands on a compatible stigma, carried by wind, water, or an animal.
Pollen tubeThe pollen grain grows a tube through the style toward an ovule.
Two sperm arriveThe tube carries sperm cells to the female gametophyte.
Double fertilizationOne sperm joins the egg (2n zygote); the other joins polar nuclei (food-storing endosperm).
Seed and fruitThe ovule becomes a seed; the ovary usually develops into a fruit.

The embryo grows from the zygote. Its food supply may be stored in the endosperm or cotyledons. Seeds can stay dormant until cues such as water, temperature, or light indicate that conditions are favorable.

Fruits protect seeds and help move them to new locations. Some fruits travel by wind or water; others are eaten by animals, which may later deposit the seeds elsewhere.

Sexual and asexual

Two ways flowering plants reproduce

StrategyHow it worksTrade-off
Sexual reproductionMeiosis and fertilization combine genetic material from gametes.Creates genetic variation, which can help populations adapt; finding a mate or pollinator may take time.
Asexual / vegetative reproductionNew plants grow from stems, roots, leaves, bulbs, runners, or other plant tissue. Some plants can also form seeds without fertilization through apomixis.Can reproduce quickly without a mate, but offspring are genetically very similar to the parent.

Plants have ways to reduce self-fertilization. Some have male and female flowers on separate plants; others release pollen and become receptive at different times. In self-incompatibility, molecular recognition can reject pollen that carries matching incompatibility alleles.

People also clone plants from cuttings or tissue culture. Grafting joins a shoot (scion) to a rooted plant (stock); it combines useful traits in one plant but does not create a new species.

Changing crop traits

Selective breeding and genetic engineering

Plant breeding changes populations over generations by choosing parents with useful inherited traits. In hybridization, breeders cross varieties or sometimes related species, then select offspring that combine desirable features such as yield, flavor, or disease resistance.

MethodWhat changes?Example / consideration
Selective breedingPeople choose which plants reproduce; many genes can change over generations.Used for thousands of years to make crops with desired traits.
Genetic engineeringResearchers add, remove, or edit particular DNA sequences using biotechnology.Bt maize carries a bacterial gene that helps protect it from certain insect pests; some crops are engineered for herbicide tolerance or improved nutrition.

Genetically modified (GM) plants can affect food production, pesticide use, nutrition, and farming practices. Possible benefits and risks depend on the crop and trait. Assessment should use evidence and consider effects on health, non-target organisms, gene flow to wild relatives, and farming systems rather than assuming all GM crops are identical.

Science thinking: A trait’s effect depends on where and how a gene is expressed, the organism’s environment, and how people use the crop. Scientific testing looks for intended effects and unexpected ones.

Researchers also study ways to limit the movement of engineered genes, such as managing pollen, using crop varieties that do not produce viable pollen, or targeting DNA to chloroplasts when inheritance patterns make that useful.

Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. What does each flower part do?

Sepals protect buds; petals can attract pollinators; stamens make pollen; carpels receive pollen and contain ovules.

2. What are the two products of double fertilization?

A diploid zygote that develops into the embryo, and endosperm that stores food for the embryo.

3. Why can asexual reproduction be useful? What is its trade-off?

It can make many offspring quickly without a mate, but produces less genetic variation than sexual reproduction.

4. How does genetic engineering differ from selective breeding?

Selective breeding chooses parents and reshapes many traits across generations. Genetic engineering directly changes specific DNA sequences with biotechnology.

  • Trace pollen from stigma to ovule and explain double fertilization.
  • Explain how ovules, seeds, ovaries, and fruits are related.
  • Compare sexual and asexual reproduction.
  • Describe one benefit and one question scientists evaluate for a GM crop.
