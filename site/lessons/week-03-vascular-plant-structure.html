How a Plant Is Built

The whole plant

Organs work as a connected system

A plant organ contains multiple tissues working together. The root system anchors the plant, takes up water and minerals, and often stores food. The shoot system includes stems, leaves, and flowers. Stems hold leaves toward light and connect roots to shoots; leaves capture light and exchange gases.

Organ or featureStructure cluesMain functions
RootRoot cap protects the tip; root hairs increase surface area. Taproot systems have a main root; fibrous systems have many similar-sized roots.Anchor, absorb water and minerals, conduct materials, and sometimes store sugars.
StemNodes are where leaves attach; internodes are stem sections between nodes. Axillary buds can grow into branches.Support leaves and flowers; connect organs and transport water, minerals, and sugars.
LeafBlade provides broad surface; petiole connects it to a stem. Veins contain vascular tissue.Capture light, exchange carbon dioxide and oxygen, and regulate water loss through stomata.
Three tissue systems

Dermal, vascular, and ground tissue

Dermal

The plant’s outer covering. The epidermis protects; a waxy cuticle helps reduce water loss. Roots have absorptive surfaces; leaves have stomata and guard cells.

Vascular

Xylem and phloem form continuous transport pathways through roots, stems, and leaves.

Ground

Fills much of the space between dermal and vascular tissue. It carries out photosynthesis, storage, and support.

Cell types and their jobs
Cell typeWhere / what it does
ParenchymaLiving, thin-walled cells; carry out much of a plant’s metabolism and storage. Some can divide and help repair tissue.
CollenchymaLiving cells with unevenly thickened walls; support young stems and leaves while they are still growing.
SclerenchymaCells with thick, lignified walls; fibers and sclereids strengthen mature parts. Many are dead when fully developed.
Xylem cellsTracheids and vessel elements conduct water and minerals. Their thick walls help prevent collapse; they are dead at functional maturity.
Phloem cellsSieve-tube elements conduct sugars. In angiosperms, companion cells help maintain and regulate sieve tubes.
Quick contrast: Xylem carries mostly water and dissolved minerals from roots toward shoots. Phloem carries sugars from sources (often mature leaves) to sinks (growing or storing tissues). We’ll study the transport mechanisms in Week 4.

Most leaves balance two needs that can conflict: letting carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis and limiting water loss. Stomata and their guard cells help manage that trade-off.

Where new cells come from

Meristems make plant growth possible

Meristems are regions of actively dividing cells. Their young cells can specialize as a plant grows.

Growth typeWhat changes?Where it happens
Primary growthRoots and shoots grow longer.Apical meristems at root and shoot tips. New nodes, internodes, leaves, and root tissues form.
Secondary growthStems and roots grow wider.Lateral meristems—vascular cambium and cork cambium—especially in woody plants.

A root cap protects the root apical meristem as it pushes through soil. In shoots, apical buds contain the shoot apical meristem; axillary buds can form side branches. Monocot stems often have scattered vascular bundles, while eudicot stems commonly arrange them in a ring.

Woody plants

How a stem adds rings

The vascular cambium makes secondary xylem toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside. Secondary xylem accumulates as wood. In many climates, seasonal changes create visible growth rings: wider growth often forms during favorable conditions, narrower growth during less favorable conditions.

Older inner xylem that no longer conducts water is heartwood; younger outer xylem that still conducts is sapwood. The cork cambium makes protective tissue; cork and associated tissues form the periderm. “Bark” refers to tissues outside the vascular cambium, including phloem and periderm.

One-way sequence: primary growth adds length; secondary growth adds thickness. A tree trunk expands because lateral meristems keep producing new tissue.
Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. How do the three tissue systems differ?

Dermal covers and protects; vascular transports water, minerals, and sugars; ground tissue carries out much of photosynthesis, storage, and support.

2. Which plant cells conduct water? Are they alive at maturity?

Tracheids and vessel elements in xylem conduct water. They are dead at functional maturity and have strengthened walls.

3. What is the difference between primary and secondary growth?

Primary growth lengthens roots and shoots from apical meristems. Secondary growth widens roots and stems through lateral meristems.

4. Why do leaves have air spaces and stomata?

Air spaces help gases diffuse through the leaf; stomata let carbon dioxide enter and allow the plant to regulate water-vapor loss.

  • Identify root, stem, and leaf structures and connect each to its function.
  • Compare dermal, vascular, and ground tissues.
  • Explain what vascular and cork cambia produce.
  • Distinguish primary growth from secondary growth.
