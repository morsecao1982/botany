How Plants Sense and Respond

Plants cannot move away from a changing environment, so they use hormones, light sensors, and defense systems to adjust growth and respond to pathogens and herbivores.

A response has steps

Signal → response

A plant cell can sense a hormone, light, gravity, touch, or a stress. In a signal-transduction pathway, a receptor detects a signal, relay steps pass the information through the cell, and target processes change. One signal can activate many molecules, amplifying a small message into a strong response.

ReceptionA receptor detects a chemical or environmental signal.
TransductionRelay proteins and second messengers carry and amplify the information.
ResponseCell activity changes—for example, a gene is switched on or cells grow differently.

Hormones are chemical signals made in one part of a plant and active in the same or another part. Their effect depends on the tissue, the concentration, and other signals present.

Plant chemical messages

Hormones coordinate growth and stress responses

Auxin helps explain phototropism: when light comes from one side, auxin distribution changes so cells on the shaded side elongate more, bending the shoot toward the light.

Light is information

Plants measure direction, color, and day length

  • Phototropism: shoot growth bends toward a light source. Leaves can turn to capture more light.
  • Phytochrome: a pigment switches between forms in response to red and far-red light. It helps seeds detect light conditions and plants detect shade and season.
  • Photoperiodism: responses to day and night length. Many plants use night length as a seasonal cue for flowering. Short-day plants flower when nights exceed a critical length; long-day plants flower when nights are shorter than a critical length.
  • Circadian rhythms: internal cycles near 24 hours, reset by daily cues such as dawn and dusk.

Light quality beneath a canopy can tell a plant that taller neighbors are nearby. Shade-avoidance responses may cause a stem to grow taller to reach sunlight.

Gravity, touch, and weather

Responses to other signals

StimulusResponse example
GravityRoots grow down (positive gravitropism); shoots grow up (negative gravitropism). Starch-filled statoliths help some root cells sense gravity.
TouchTendrils coil around supports (thigmotropism); some leaves move rapidly after touch.
DroughtABA helps close stomata, conserving water but slowing CO2 intake.
FloodingRoots may form air channels that help oxygen move through waterlogged tissues.
Heat or coldCells adjust membranes and produce stress-response proteins that help protect other proteins.
SaltPlants adjust internal solutes to reduce water loss to salty surroundings.

Plants also detect wounds and changes in temperature, water, and chemicals. Their responses can change growth, metabolism, and gene activity.

Pathogens and herbivores

Plants defend themselves

A pathogen is an organism or virus that causes disease. Plant pathogens include fungi, bacteria, viruses, and fungus-like oomycetes. Because plants cannot escape, they use physical barriers, chemical defenses, and cell-to-cell warning systems.

Before infection

Waxy cuticle, cell walls, bark, thorns, and leaf hairs (trichomes) make entry or feeding harder. Some plants make bitter or toxic compounds.

At infection

Cells recognize common pathogen molecules and activate local defenses. A hypersensitive response can kill infected cells and form a lesion that limits spread.

Across the plant

Signals such as salicylic acid can alert distant tissues, producing systemic acquired resistance for a period after infection.

Against herbivores

Jasmonate signaling can increase chemical defenses and may attract predators or parasitoids that attack the herbivore.

Spotting a lesion: A dead or discolored patch can mark where the plant sealed off infected tissue. But not every spot is an infection; drought, minerals, insects, and fungi can produce overlapping symptoms.

Disease can spread when infected plants or wood move to new locations. Potato blight caused famine in Ireland in the 1840s; chestnut blight and sudden oak death changed forests. Wild relatives of crops can preserve genes that may help breeders respond to future diseases.

Fungi and plants

Partners can help—or cause disease

Fungi are not plants. They are heterotrophs that digest food outside their bodies and absorb the products. Many grow as branching filaments called hyphae; a network of hyphae is a mycelium.

RelationshipWhat happensExample
MycorrhizaMutualism: fungal hyphae help roots absorb minerals and water; plants give the fungus sugars.Common in most vascular plants.
EndophyteA fungus lives inside plant tissue without harming it; some help protect the plant from herbivores or stress.Fungi inside grasses and leaves.
PathogenA fungus or fungus-like organism takes resources from a host and causes disease.Chestnut blight is caused by a fungus; sudden oak death is caused by an oomycete.

Some fungi are essential decomposers and others are useful in food or medicine. Their effects on plants range from partnership to disease, so identify the interaction rather than assuming every fungus is harmful.

Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. What are the three steps of signal transduction?

Reception of a signal, transduction through relay steps, and a cellular response.

2. Which hormone is especially important when a plant is drought-stressed?

Abscisic acid (ABA), which helps close stomata and conserve water.

3. Why can infected leaves develop spots or lesions?

A hypersensitive response can isolate the infection by killing cells around it, depriving the pathogen of resources and limiting spread.

4. How is a mycorrhiza different from a fungal disease?

A mycorrhiza is a mutualistic partnership that benefits both fungus and plant; a pathogen harms its host.

  • Match plant hormones to major effects.
  • Explain phototropism, gravitropism, and photoperiodism.
  • Compare physical, local, and systemic defenses.
  • Recognize mutualistic, harmless, and pathogenic plant-fungus relationships.
