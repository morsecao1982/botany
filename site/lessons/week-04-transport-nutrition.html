How Plants Move Water, Sugars, and Minerals

Trace water from soil to leaf, follow sugar from source to sink, and see how soil minerals and helpful microbes support plant growth.

Getting resources

Roots explore a living soil

Soil is a mixture of weathered rock, water, air, dead organic matter, and living organisms. Its topsoil often has the most organic material and biological activity. Roots absorb water and dissolved mineral ions, especially through root hairs, which increase surface area.

Plants need chemical elements to build molecules and run cell processes. Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen come mainly from carbon dioxide and water. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and sulfur are needed in larger amounts; other elements such as iron, zinc, boron, and molybdenum are needed in smaller amounts.

Deficiency patterns are clues, not diagnoses by themselves. Symptoms vary with species, soil, and which nutrient is missing. Look at which leaves are affected, where discoloration begins, and whether growth points are damaged.

A nutrient can be present in soil but unavailable to roots because of pH, waterlogging, or how it binds to soil particles. Farmers use fertilizers and soil management to replace depleted nutrients, but excess nutrients can wash into waterways.

Across short distances

How water enters and crosses roots

Water moves by osmosis across selectively permeable membranes. Water potential combines the effects of dissolved solutes and physical pressure to predict which way water tends to move. Water flows from higher water potential toward lower water potential.

Apoplast route

Water moves through cell walls and spaces outside plasma membranes. The endodermis blocks this route at the root’s vascular cylinder.

Symplast route

Water moves through cytoplasm from cell to cell, including through plasmodesmata. It has crossed a plasma membrane at least once.

The endodermis acts as a checkpoint. Its Casparian strip forces water and dissolved ions to cross a cell membrane before entering the xylem, allowing the plant to control which substances enter the transport stream. When plant cells take up water, pressure against their walls makes them turgid; when they lose too much water, they become flaccid and the plant may wilt.

Root to leaf

Transpiration pulls water through xylem

Transpiration is the loss of water vapor, mainly through stomata. As water evaporates from leaf cell walls, it lowers water potential and creates tension in the water column inside xylem. Water molecules cohere to one another through hydrogen bonds and adhere to xylem walls, helping the column stay connected as it is pulled upward.

Guard cells regulate stomata. When they take up potassium ions and water, they become more turgid and the pore opens. In drought, abscisic acid (ABA) helps trigger stomatal closing. Closing reduces water loss but also slows carbon dioxide entry and photosynthesis.

Moving sugars

Phloem carries sugars from source to sink

Sources release sugars into phloem; mature photosynthetic leaves are common sources. Sinks use or store sugars; growing roots, fruits, seeds, and young leaves are examples. A plant organ can be a source at one time and a sink at another.

In the pressure-flow model, sugar loading into sieve tubes draws in water from nearby xylem. The higher pressure pushes phloem sap toward a lower-pressure sink, where sugar is unloaded and used or stored. Unlike xylem flow, phloem flow can go in different directions in different sieve tubes, depending on where sources and sinks are.

Memory hook: Xylem’s water stream is pulled mainly by evaporation at leaves. Phloem sap moves by pressure differences created by loading and unloading sugars.
Nutrition is a community effort

Microbes and unusual plant strategies

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria

Rhizobium bacteria live in legume root nodules. They convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms; the plant supplies sugars. Crop rotation with legumes can add nitrogen to soil.

Mycorrhizae

Fungal hyphae connected to roots increase the area for absorbing water and minerals, especially phosphorus. The plant supplies sugars in return.

Epiphytes and parasites

Epiphytes grow on another plant but gather their own water and minerals. Parasitic plants tap into a host for water, minerals, or sugars.

Carnivorous plants

Photosynthesize, but trap insects to supplement minerals such as nitrogen in nutrient-poor habitats.

Some soil bacteria also make nutrients more available, and fungi can protect roots from pathogens. These relationships affect plant growth, agriculture, and the structure of plant communities.

Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. What makes water move from soil into a root cell?

Water moves down a water-potential gradient across selectively permeable membranes; dissolved solutes and pressure both affect water potential.

2. What is transpiration, and how does it help move water upward?

Transpiration is water-vapor loss from leaves. Evaporation creates tension that pulls a cohesive water column up xylem.

3. How are sources and sinks different?

Sources release sugars to phloem; sinks use or store them. Phloem sap moves from source toward sink.

4. What do plants and mycorrhizal fungi exchange?

The fungus helps deliver water and minerals; the plant supplies sugars.

  • Trace water from soil, through root tissues, into xylem, and out a leaf.
  • Compare xylem and phloem using what each transports and how it moves.
  • Recognize common deficiency patterns while remembering that visual symptoms can overlap.
  • Explain how bacteria and fungi can improve plant nutrition.
