Plants in Communities and Ecosystems

Study competition, herbivory, and mutualism in plant communities, then follow energy and nutrients through ecosystems.

Many species, shared places

What makes a community?

An ecological community is the set of populations of different species living and interacting in the same area. Community ecologists study how these interactions affect where species live, how abundant they are, and how communities change over time.

A species’ fundamental niche is the range of conditions and resources it could use without competitors. Its realized niche is where it actually lives after interactions such as competition limit it.

Who affects whom?

Competition, herbivory, and helpful partnerships

InteractionEffect on each partnerPlant example
Competition− / −: both are harmed by using the same limited resource.Two plants compete for light, water, space, or soil nutrients.
Herbivory+ / −: the herbivore benefits; the plant is harmed.Caterpillars eat leaves; mammals graze grasses.
Mutualism+ / +: both partners benefit.Pollinators transfer pollen; mycorrhizal fungi help roots take up minerals.
Commensalism+ / 0: one benefits, and the other is not measurably affected.An epiphyte uses a tree for support without taking its food.
Parasitism+ / −: the parasite benefits while harming its host.A parasitic plant taps a host for water, minerals, or sugars.

When two species depend on the same limited resource, one may outcompete the other in a particular place. Competitive exclusion describes the outcome when one species prevents another from occupying the same niche indefinitely. Species can coexist by partitioning resources—using different foods, places, or times of day.

Competition can also shape traits over generations. If similar species live together, natural selection may favor differences in how they use resources. Interactions are not always fixed: a relationship can shift between neutral and beneficial depending on conditions.

Herbivores influence plant communities by feeding on leaves, stems, seeds, and roots. Plants may defend themselves with thorns, hairs, or chemicals; herbivores may specialize on plants they can tolerate. Plants also alter their surroundings: a salt-marsh plant can shade soil and lower evaporation, making conditions more favorable for neighboring plants.

Energy moves through

Food webs connect producers to consumers

Plants and photosynthetic algae are producers: they bring light energy into food webs by storing it in organic molecules. Herbivores are primary consumers; predators are higher-level consumers. Detritus—dead organisms, fallen leaves, and waste—feeds detritivores and decomposers such as fungi and bacteria.

SunlightEnergy enters when producers photosynthesize.
Plant tissueStored chemical energy feeds herbivores and people.
ConsumersEnergy moves through feeding relationships.
DecomposersBreak down remains; nutrients return to soil and water.

Energy is transferred and transformed, with much released as heat at each step. It flows through an ecosystem and must be continually supplied. Nutrient atoms, by contrast, can be reused and cycled. Because only a portion of energy becomes new biomass at each level, food chains usually have fewer top predators than producers.

Matter cycles through

Plants shape carbon and nutrient cycles

CyclePlant roleKey idea
CarbonPhotosynthesis takes CO2 from air or water and stores carbon in sugars and plant tissue. Respiration, decomposition, and burning return carbon.Forests and soils can store carbon; deforestation and fossil-fuel burning add CO2 to the atmosphere.
NitrogenPlants absorb ammonium or nitrate and build nitrogen into proteins and DNA. Some bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen; legume root nodules partner with nitrogen-fixing bacteria.Decomposition returns nitrogen; other bacteria change its form. Fertilizers and runoff can disrupt the cycle.
PhosphorusPlants take up phosphate and use it in DNA, membranes, and ATP.Weathering releases phosphate from rocks; it moves through soil, water, and organisms. It has little movement through the atmosphere.
WaterRoots absorb water; transpiration releases it from leaves.Evaporation, precipitation, runoff, and plant uptake move water between land, air, and oceans.

In a forest study at Hubbard Brook, researchers found that cutting trees increased water runoff and sharply increased nitrate leaving the watershed. Intact plants had been absorbing water and nutrients. Excess nutrients in downstream waters can stimulate algal blooms and lower oxygen levels.

Applying ecology

Why these interactions matter

Communities support pollination, seed dispersal, soil formation, nutrient cycling, and food production. Changing one important species can affect many others. Restoration ecology uses this knowledge to repair damaged ecosystems—for example, by restoring native plants, reconnecting habitats, and rebuilding soil processes.

Keep the distinction clear: Energy flows through food webs and is gradually lost as heat. Matter—carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water—cycles among organisms and the physical environment.
Retrieval practice

Check yourself

1. What does a − / − interaction mean?

Both species are harmed. Competition is − / − because each uses resources that would otherwise be available to the other.

2. How can two competing plants coexist?

They may partition resources—for example, by using different soil depths, light levels, or seasons.

3. Why does energy flow while nutrients cycle?

Energy is transformed and lost as heat at each transfer, so it needs a continuing source. Nutrient atoms are reused through feeding, waste, decomposition, and uptake.

4. What happened to nitrate runoff after deforestation at Hubbard Brook?

It increased greatly because fewer plants remained to take up water and nutrients.

  • Explain fundamental and realized niches and resource partitioning.
  • Trace energy from producers through consumers and decomposers.
  • Describe how plants participate in carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water cycles.
